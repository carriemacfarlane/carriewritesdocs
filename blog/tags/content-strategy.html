Capitalization

November 1, 2026 · 2 min read
Carrie M. Macfarlane
Technical Writer

The new Strategic Naming Team was halfway through its first assignment. We were choosing a name for a feature that completes the first step of the customer billing process. We had identified recurring assessments as the core concept, so that’s what we’d call it. But should we capitalize recurring assessments?

Our goal was to choose a name that could be used consistently across documentation, support, and advertising. “Turn on the setting that allows you to automatically start the billing process every month” is a mouthful. No one would say all those words every time! “Turn on recurring assessments” (or, “Turn on Recurring Assessments”!) was concise and repeatable.

All features have to be called something, and capitalized names get noticed. But as the Intuit design system points out, if all features are capitalized, the user and the brand suffers. Imagine a paragraph where capital letters interrupt every sentence. It’s frustrating! When all features are capitalized, the impact of the most valuable features is weakened.

I appreciate systems designer Scott Kubie’s reminder that sometimes a chair is just a chair, not an Eames Lounger. But when our devs have worked really hard on a new feature, how can we tell that it's an Eames Lounger? Sometimes we realize only after the excitement of release that what we had was just a chair.

The Strategic Naming Team applied a style guide rule based on the Intuit guide: noteworthy features that help users complete significant tasks are capitalized, and commonplace features that help users complete minor tasks are not. The new feature helps some users complete one step in the billing process. Our marketing team didn’t plan to publicize it. This means it’s a minor task, and it’s not noteworthy. It’s recurring assessments.

What I’m Working On​

  • Capitalization of feature names.

Question​

  • When should a feature name be capitalized?

Answer​

  • Noteworthy features that help users complete significant tasks are capitalized. Commonplace features that help users complete minor tasks are not capitalized.

Citations​

  • Formatting | Intuit
  • Fighting Proper Noun Feature Names | Scott Kubie

Terminology List with AI

August 27, 2026 · 2 min read
Carrie M. Macfarlane
Technical Writer

I’ve cleared some space in my schedule to work on content strategy. There’s lots to do, so I’m going to try to enlist AI. I started a subscription to Claude Pro, and I plan to tell my department how it goes. Can Claude Pro help with content strategy?

Inspired by Tara English-Sweeney, who used AI for translation-type work, and by Tom Johnson, who used AI to build a glossary, the first area I'm exploring is terminology management.

I have a 3 year-old glossary of user interface elements. It’s become a long and unwieldy Google doc. I've now spent several afternoons iterating with Claude. After taking a few regrettable trips down rabbit holes, I've built a proof-of-concept spreadsheet.

A key feature of the spreadsheet is identifiers. Every term has a unique and permanent ID. This should make it easier for me to manage terminology changes over time. The ID, along with parent and navigation columns, also should allow writers to confirm they're using current names.

I like this new format, and despite my librarian superpowers, I probably would have needed more than several afternoons to come up with it on my own. My next step is to test the spreadsheet with a larger term set. If everything checks out, I’ll share it with the team for feedback.

What I’m Working On​

  • Using AI for content strategy work.

Question​

  • How can Claude Pro help?

Answer​

  • By converting my glossary of user interface elements from a long Google Doc into a compact spreadsheet.

Content Strategy

June 13, 2026 · 3 min read
Carrie M. Macfarlane
Technical Writer

I recently passed my 3-year work anniversary and I’ve decided it’s time to update my job description. In addition to writing, editing, and mentoring, I’ve been managing terminology, creating workflows, writing user interface text, and initiating cross-team conversations about content alignment. What I’ve been doing is content strategy, but I’ve never heard that term used in my office. To get buy-in for this revision, I’ll have to talk up content strategy. I’m looking for a soundbite that explains what content strategy is.

Years ago, when I started exploring technical writing, I listened every week to a podcast from content strategist Kristina Halvorson. She talked about a content strategy “quad.” The quad was split into two halves, content and people. Substance and structure components made up the content half, and workflow and governance components made up the people half. If you pointed to the quad, you could quickly describe content strategy as a combination of content work and people work.

Kristina has shared an updated content strategy quad on her blog. The new quad highlights different kinds of work: a content design half contains editorial and experience components, and a systems design half contains structure and process components. So now, you could quickly describe content strategy as a combination of content design work and systems design work.

Both the old quad and the new quad describe the work I do, but the new quad is a better fit. My colleagues are used to hearing me talk about words. Now I need them to notice that I'm also doing systems work. So, I’ll explain that content strategy is a combination of content design and systems design. It connects our content work with our business goals.

What I’m Working On​

  • Updating my job description to include content strategy.

Question​

  • What’s a good way to describe content strategy? I need to make it easy for my boss and the rest of the company to start using the term.

Answer​

  • Content strategy is a combination of content design and systems design. It connects content work with business goals.

  • What Is Content Strategy? Connecting the Dots Between Disciplines | Brain Traffic
